Role IDs, module IDs, decision IDs, risk IDs and gate IDs are reusable keys for procurement, staffing, engineering tickets, test cases and formal signoffs.
1. Scope and interpretation
The proposed product combines independent subsidiary governance, enterprise DMS/KMS functionality, optional Zero-Knowledge content, records controls, AI-assisted discovery and multilingual interfaces. Its trust model includes a WordPress integration boundary and an independent document/identity service. A WordPress administrator is NOT by default a repository administrator or decryption authority.
Required interpretation: An implementation role is an accountable discipline, not necessarily a distinct full-time employee. A single team member may cover several compatible roles; independent assessors, key trustees, and conflict-of-interest checks must not be collapsed into the people they verify.
Assessment method: Reviewed the 22 module headings and 346 feature IDs in the supplied blueprint; mapped required specialists to module ownership, cryptographic/authorization consequences, operating roles, evidence, and phase gates.
Risk vocabulary: Blocker = cannot claim high-assurance production readiness until resolved. High = address before affected feature is enabled or formally govern residual risk. Medium = planned control and tracked verification. No software can be promised defect-free.
Core architectural constraint: Zero-Knowledge contents cannot be meaningfully processed by ordinary server-side OCR, AI, converters or malware scanners without exposing plaintext or adopting a different threat model. The feature availability matrix must be enforced, not implied.
Scope boundaries: Local regulatory regimes, deployment country, number and sensitivity of users, identity-provider choice, data volumes, hosting model, security certifications, procurement contracts, and numerical SLOs still require sponsor signoff.

A practical initial core team has a dedicated product owner, technical architect, delivery manager, business analyst, backend/API engineers, WordPress engineer, web/client engineers, platform/DevOps, test automation, UX and security engineering. Separation of duties must be operationally credible. In particular, (a) the person who implements cryptography does not issue the only cryptographic assurance report; (b) release engineers cannot unilaterally override security go/no-go; (c) the holding administrator is not a recovery trustee merely because of seniority; and (d) the document author cannot approve their own controlled publication.

Authorization semantics to implement exactly
Identifiers: Immutable principal_id, tenant_id, group_id, document_id and document_version_id, never guessed from a client-selected tenant alone.
Evaluation order: Authentication + device status → tenant membership → mandatory group and legal holds → document classification and ACL/ABAC → context and token caveats → permit or deny; explicit deny wins.
Policy timing: Authorization is re-evaluated for each sensitive operation, background export, preview, key-wrap issuance, restore and potentially long-running job.
Administrator separation: Ability to manage a tenant or rotate a system certificate is not equivalent to ability to read documents, remove holds or reconstruct private keys.
Impersonation and emergency access: No silent impersonation. Time-bounded emergency operations require approved reason, dual control, exact scope, independent logging and post-incident review.
Revocation reality: Revoke future sessions, grants and key distribution, but disclose that earlier plaintext or keys copied outside the service may persist.

6. Role-led assessment of every feature module
Each entry states the additional engineering design considerations that the specialist team must resolve beyond the existence of the named 346 baseline requirements. Modules marked Critical require an explicit pre-production demonstration, not only a feature walkthrough.
TEN  |  Tenant governance / subsidiaries  (14 features)
Risk: Critical. Accountable: R02. Build: R07, R29, R31. Consult: R11, R12, R21, R19.
Design considerations: Model legal company boundaries, groups and project spaces separately; prohibit implicit holding-admin document access; apply group minimum policies without silent privilege expansion; isolate DB, object storage, queues, search, logs, backups, and caches; design mergers/splits as supervised migrations.
Minimum acceptance evidence: A cross-tenant user, cache key, search request, export job or backup restore never reveals unauthorized tenant material.
IAM  |  Membership, roles and policy  (17 features)
Risk: Critical. Accountable: R21. Build: R29, R31, R30. Consult: R02, R19, R11, R27.
Design considerations: Formalize RBAC + ABAC + ACL evaluation order, global hard-deny, explicit deny, inheritance and policy versioning; enforce separation of duties; prevent self-grants and delegation loops; invalidate permissions in jobs, URLs and offline clients; provide decision explanations without leaking titles.
Minimum acceptance evidence: For each permission state, API, previews, exports, mobile, workers and search enforce the same decision after revocation.
AUT  |  Authentication and devices  (13 features)
Risk: Critical. Accountable: R21. Build: R36, R37, R29. Consult: R19, R22, R23, R14.
Design considerations: Use enterprise IdP and phishing-resistant factors for privileged access; separate WordPress and document-service sessions; map IdP identity to immutable tenant memberships; lifecycle-sync join/move/leave; sign and verify E2EE device public keys; specify lost-device and IdP-outage handling.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Compromised WordPress cookies cannot mint a document-system privileged identity or enroll an unapproved E2EE device.
DOC  |  Document capture and lifecycle  (22 features)
Risk: Critical. Accountable: R03. Build: R32, R29, R31. Consult: R12, R19, R20, R11.
Design considerations: Version documents immutably with content integrity and provenance; quarantine new uploads; validate type by content; sandbox all converters; make document metadata, previews and exports classification-aware; guarantee idempotency and quota enforcement on resumable uploads.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Malformed file, aborted upload, version race or failed scan cannot expose partially processed plaintext or bypass approval.
MET  |  Metadata, taxonomy and search  (16 features)
Risk: High. Accountable: R15. Build: R34, R31, R29. Consult: R20, R14, R17, R19.
Design considerations: Version metadata schemas and taxonomies; specify encrypted/public fields and side-channel leakage budgets; tenant-scope autosuggest, indexes, caches and analytics; support locale-specific word segmentation; keep E2EE content search on trusted clients or explicitly approved leakage-limited protocols.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Search returns only authorized resources and cannot reveal confidential filenames, snippets or existence through autocomplete.
COL  |  Editing and collaboration  (16 features)
Risk: High. Accountable: R03. Build: R30, R29, R37. Consult: R20, R12, R19, R18.
Design considerations: Separate draft collaboration from immutable approved record; resolve locks, autosaves, failed merges and approvals; sandbox third-party editors; make E2EE collaborative editing a separate approved protocol; treat watermarks and screenshot controls as deterrents, not guarantees.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Unauthorized reviewer cannot change an approved hash or gain editor plaintext through preview/editor callback.
KNO  |  Knowledge management / wiki  (16 features)
Risk: High. Accountable: R15. Build: R30, R29, R34. Consult: R11, R12, R17, R18.
Design considerations: Assign content steward and periodic validation; separate canonical policies from discussion threads; enforce link traversal rights; maintain multilingual article versions and fallback markers; prove read-and-attest evidence binds a specific version and language.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Users see only permitted linked knowledge; acknowledgment is linked to exact approved revision, not mutable draft.
WFL  |  Workflow and approvals  (19 features)
Risk: High. Accountable: R03. Build: R33, R29, R30. Consult: R21, R12, R13, R19.
Design considerations: Design versioned state machines with explicit retry/idempotency semantics; enforce maker/checker, approval quorum and delegated-authority limits; bind approval to document hash/policy; protect confidential notification payload; distinguish internal approval from legally qualified e-signature.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Workflow cannot be self-approved through race conditions, delegation cycles, replayed jobs or version swaps.
REC  |  Records, holds, eDiscovery  (18 features)
Risk: Critical. Accountable: R12. Build: R29, R31, R32. Consult: R13, R14, R20, R27.
Design considerations: Capture jurisdictional retention rules, classification events, WORM scopes and legal hold precedence; define conflicts with subject erasure, backups and cryptographic deletion; assess whether E2EE discovery requires trustee-based access or custodian-driven client export.
Minimum acceptance evidence: Hold blocks destructive operations across versions, exports, backup expiry and crypto-erasure until lawfully released.
ENC  |  Encryption / keys / E2EE  (20 features)
Risk: Critical. Accountable: R20. Build: R37, R32, R29. Consult: R25, R19, R21, R26.
Design considerations: Independent trusted code distribution must withstand a WordPress breach; use reviewed AEAD and per-version keys, authenticated document context, verified recipient identities, rekey and recovery models; explicitly expose recovery-vs-zero-access trade-off; test WordPress takeover.
Minimum acceptance evidence: External reviewers reproduce crypto vectors; simulated WordPress RCE cannot decrypt protected records or forge recipients.

7. Architecture and policy conflicts requiring deliberate trade-offs
The system cannot safely promise every capability in both encryption modes. These conflicts should become a published mode/feature compatibility matrix and explicit policy warnings in configuration screens.
CONFLICT 01  ZERO-KNOWLEDGE FILES VS SERVER OCR/SEARCH/AI
Design resolution: Use trusted-client processing; otherwise managed mode after explicit classification approval.
Cross-functional owners: R20 / R34 / R35
CONFLICT 02  ZERO-KNOWLEDGE MALWARE INSPECTION VS OPAQUE CIPHERTEXT
Design resolution: Admit reduced server assurance; client scan, device posture, staging/quarantine and policy restrictions.
Cross-functional owners: R19 / R32
CONFLICT 03  STRONG CONFIDENTIALITY VS FORGOTTEN KEYS
Design resolution: Choose no recovery (permanent loss possible) or independently governed quorum recovery (extra access path).
Cross-functional owners: R20 / R26
CONFLICT 04  AUTHORIZATION REVOCATION VS OFFLINE ACCESS
Design resolution: Revoke future delivery; clearly disclose inability to erase previously copied plaintext or keys.
Cross-functional owners: R21 / R37
CONFLICT 05  GROUP CENTRAL OVERSIGHT VS SUBSIDIARY CONFIDENTIALITY
Design resolution: Keep control-plane and document-key permissions separate; aggregated group reporting.
Cross-functional owners: R02 / R21
CONFLICT 06  LEGAL HOLD VS RIGHT TO ERASURE / CRYPTO ERASURE
Design resolution: Apply jurisdiction-specific priority with recorded legal justification, review and execution gates.
Cross-functional owners: R12 / R13 / R14
CONFLICT 07  E2EE VS COURT-DIRECTED EDISCOVERY
Design resolution: Adopt legal custodian export or trustee recovery procedure; do not imply invisible server decryption.
Cross-functional owners: R13 / R20
CONFLICT 08  BROWSER CLIENT E2EE VS COMPROMISED CODE DELIVERY
Design resolution: Use independently hosted signed client and verification; do not trust JavaScript supplied by WordPress.
Cross-functional owners: R20 / R37
CONFLICT 09  ENTERPRISE COAUTHORING VS E2EE

Cross-functional owners: R35 / R21

Cross-functional owners: R27 / R14
CONFLICT 12  DIGITAL APPROVAL VS LEGAL ELECTRONIC SIGNATURE

Cross-functional owners: R13 / R33
CONFLICT 13  THIRD-PARTY EDITOR VS CONTENT CONFIDENTIALITY

CONFLICT 14  PUBLIC KB PUBLISHING VS HIDDEN TENANT CONTENT

Cross-functional owners: R15 / R19

12. High-value cross-functional acceptance tests
TEST 01 — ISOLATION  [TEN,IAM,API,RPT]
Attack or operational scenario: Tenant A admin guesses Tenant B document/version/metadata/queue ID
Expected proof: All paths deny and independent audit has evidence.
TEST 02 — E2EE CODE INTEGRITY  [ENC,API,MOB]
Attack or operational scenario: WordPress administrator modifies WP theme/plugin to serve malicious JS
Expected proof: Trusted client neither executes injected code nor releases keys or plaintext.
TEST 03 — RECIPIENT AUTHENTICITY  [ENC,AUT,EXT]
Attack or operational scenario: Attacker substitutes public key in key directory
Expected proof: Trusted client blocks unverified recipient and alerts owner.
TEST 04 — PRIVILEGE ESCALATION  [IAM,TEN]
Attack or operational scenario: Subsidiary admin grants self group-wide auditor/escrow role
Expected proof: Permission creation rejected; privileged change requires separate approval.
TEST 05 — PERMISSION RACE  [IAM,DOC,WFL]
Attack or operational scenario: Revoke user while export, preview, workflow and search requests run
Expected proof: Defined consistency window enforced; no fresh unauthorized copy completed after final check.
TEST 06 — MALICIOUS DOCUMENT  [DOC,SEC]
Attack or operational scenario: Upload malformed Office/PDF designed to exploit converter
Expected proof: Quarantine and isolated sandbox prevents network/file/key compromise.
TEST 07 — CONFIDENTIAL PREVIEW  [ENC,DOC,UX]
Attack or operational scenario: Request E2EE preview via ordinary WP dashboard
Expected proof: System presents independent trusted viewer; no WP origin sees plaintext.
TEST 08 — LEGAL HOLD  [REC,ENC,OPS]
Attack or operational scenario: Place hold, then attempt deletion, retention expiry and crypto-erasure
Expected proof: All destructive paths deny with preserved evidence.
TEST 09 — KEY RECOVERY  [ENC,AUT]
Attack or operational scenario: Lose device; request recovery with one trustee instead of quorum
Expected proof: Fails until independently approved quorum, then audited recovery succeeds.
TEST 10 — DEVICE COMPROMISE  [MOB,ENC,IAM]
Attack or operational scenario: User loses previously synced phone and is deprovisioned
Expected proof: New versions and future distribution denied; prior offline data limitation displayed.
TEST 11 — CROSS-COMPANY PROJECT  [EXT,TEN,IAM]
Attack or operational scenario: Tenant A shares selected file with Tenant B group
Expected proof: Named members only, approvals and keys verified, no blanket B rights.
TEST 12 — AUDIT TAMPERING  [RPT,SEC]
Attack or operational scenario: WP admin tries to delete download and authorization failure events
Expected proof: Independent audit continues to show original events and tampering alarm.
TEST 13 — AI LEAKAGE  [AIX,MET,IAM]
Attack or operational scenario: Revoked document exists in vector index and cached answer
Expected proof: Retrieval, cache and output no longer expose that source.
TEST 14 — PROMPT INJECTION  [AIX,WFL,SEC]
Attack or operational scenario: Malicious document instructs AI agent to email private file
Expected proof: No autonomous export, tool grant or workflow approval.
TEST 15 — BACKUP RESTORE  [OPS,IAM,REC]
Attack or operational scenario: Restore tenant from old snapshot including revoked roles
Expected proof: Reconciles current policy/hold/revocations before opening restored tenant.
TEST 16 — LOCALIZATION  [L10,UX]
Attack or operational scenario: Persian and Arabic RTL document sharing, approval and permission UI
Expected proof: Native validation finds no reversed affordances/IDs; screen-reader correct.
TEST 17 — MIGRATION  [MIG,IAM]
Attack or operational scenario: Import historic documents with mismatched identity and denied ACLs
Expected proof: Dry run reports exceptions; no overly broad grants; provenance preserved.
TEST 18 — COMPANY SEPARATION  [TEN,REC,ENC]
Attack or operational scenario: Split one subsidiary into two while legal hold exists
Expected proof: Approved custody, key rewrap, audit trail and retention remain valid.
TEST 19 — CLOUD EDITOR  [COL,ENC,API]
Attack or operational scenario: Attempt to open E2EE file in external online editor
Expected proof: Blocked unless approved conversion and explicit authorized disclosure.
TEST 20 — DISASTER RECOVERY  [OPS,ENC,RPT]
Attack or operational scenario: Simulate regional outage with KMS outage and independent logs
Expected proof: System follows declared RTO/RPO without fail-open; keys/audit recover.
13. Recommended implementation sequence
Discover and govern
Resolve data ownership, tenant/legal structure, deployment jurisdiction, classification and data volumes. Select independent decision-makers and counsel.
Prove boundaries before features
Build a security spike: WordPress bridge, independent identity, ten separate tenants, formal authorization checks and secure trusted-client key envelope.
Demonstrate protected document lifecycle
Implement upload, quarantine, verified versions, document grants, separate modes, signed client, audit, key recovery decision, storage and restore.
Implement records and guided user experience
Add reliable legal holds, approvals, search restricted to mode, multilingual core UX, WCAG audits and operational runbooks.
Pilot subsidiary independence
Test two or more subsidiaries with different rules, then the remaining companies in waves; include an RTL pilot, cross-company collaboration and offboarding.
Expand after independent assurance
Add full-text/AI/advanced editing, mobile sync, integrations and vertical modules only with individual threat and privacy reviews.
Do not present the full 140 baseline Core items as a small MVP. The first shippable slice should deliberately contain only the minimum working vertical flow plus the security and legal controls necessary for that flow; independent reviewers must approve expansion.

15. Final design recommendation
Use the original 346-feature catalog as product inventory; use this review as the ownership, architectural decision and release-assurance companion. Start by demonstrating that (1) a WordPress compromise cannot access Zero-Knowledge plaintext/keys, (2) subsidiary identities and policies never bypass tenant bounds, (3) legal holds/recovery/backup restore remain consistent, and (4) independent reviewers can verify claims using reproducible tests.
Product differentiation should come from independently governed subsidiaries, transparent permission explanations, honest mode-specific capabilities, strong information-governance controls and accessible multilingual operations — not a claim of absolute security or an arbitrary feature count.
